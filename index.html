Dans Huang

Notes for the AI Support Lead role · Taipei, relocating to San Francisco

I have spent seven years at one company that makes guitar amplifiers and music software, starting on the front line and ending up running support. For the last two years my main job has been the AI that handles it: what it is allowed to do, how it is measured, and who owns it when I am not the one maintaining it.

The posting asks for five things. Here is what I have actually done on each, including the parts where my answer is partial.

1.Owning a conversational AI program end to end

Two surfaces are live and I own both. An AI agent works our inbound email tickets around the clock; I designed its escalation logic on one principle, which is that authority follows the stake of the case rather than the confidence of the model. Anything involving money, warranty, or a physical shipment goes to a person. It is also forbidden from making promises about product features or about whether another department will reply, because those promises are expensive to unwind and the customer remembers who made them.

The second is a multi-agent system my colleagues talk to in Slack. Three specialist agents report into a central orchestrator, each mapped to a named human owner, so the AI's reporting lines match the team's. Every write that leaves the system passes an approval gate before it reaches a customer, a ticket, or the knowledge base.

Before those, we ran a customer-facing chatbot on our Help Center through 2025, from an external platform partner. I selected it, signed the contract, owned the relationship and the conversation design, and ended it. It shared none of the machinery above; a good deal of that machinery exists because of what that year taught me.

To be plain about the gap: no chatbot of mine is live today, and the Slack system is internal. My live customer-facing AI is email.

2.The metrics, and defending them

The most useful thing I know about containment metrics I learned from being wrong. Our chatbot reported weekly resolution in the seventies and eighties. What was actually happening: an abandoned conversation timed out, the system closed it, sent a survey, and applied a resolved tag, and the report counted that tag. A customer who read a wrong answer and gave up scored exactly the same as one we helped. Nothing short of reading real conversations separates solved from abandoned, and I have not accepted a containment or deflection number since without asking what the denominator excludes and what counts as a win.

So on the email agent I publish two numbers rather than one, both against total closed tickets: the share the AI finished (42.6% in June 2026, 44.7% in July) and the share that touched a human at any point. They are allowed to disagree. Handoff is designed in, not failed into, which makes it structurally harder to reduce than resolution is to raise, and I would be suspicious of any quarter where both moved in the flattering direction at once.

Customer satisfaction was 86.6% in July 2026 on 448 responses. We survey about two thirds of solved tickets, not all of them: a ticket that sits waiting on the customer and auto-closes does not get a survey, because asking someone to rate us on a problem we did not finish reads as a taunt. That is a choice I made, it flatters the number slightly, and I would rather explain it than have someone find it.

3.Managing people

I have interviewed around fifteen people for support roles on a process I put together myself. Support is an entry-level job here, so I screen for character rather than experience: curiosity, clear thinking, and whether someone can describe their own mistakes accurately. I got one wrong — a strong candidate who wanted a stable environment, which ours is not — so I changed the process. I now describe the pace and the disorder as bluntly as I can, close to talking the person out of it, and watch whether the interest survives. Our most recent hire came through that version and is one of the best we have had.

My real weakness as a new manager was the opposite of hiring. In my first year I let three people go, and two of those took far too long. I kept extending coaching because I wanted them to improve, and the cost of that patience was paid by the rest of the team rather than by me. What changed is that I stopped treating being liked as part of the job. Once that was clear, the hard conversations got easier.

The part I am proudest of is smaller. A frontline specialist with no technical background now owns day-to-day maintenance of the email agent. It took six months of walking him through the architecture and the reasoning behind it, then making him experiment rather than follow my instructions. I rarely have to step in. Our team went from about ten people to five over two years, with a few voluntary transfers to other departments and three performance exits, and we never backfilled — automation absorbed the queue while the people who stayed moved into AI, automation, and knowledge work.

If I had headcount here I would hire a knowledge-base owner first, because a correct and current knowledge base is most of what makes an AI agent work, and an evaluation and quality auditor second. The second role holds the first accountable. I hold the second accountable by auditing tickets myself and watching customer satisfaction, so the internal quality score can never drift from what customers actually experienced.

4.Working across functions

The clearest evidence is that other teams use our AI without asking me. Our BI team queries the Slack system directly for support data; product and e-commerce pull it into their own channels rather than asking someone in support to go dig numbers out of the ticketing system. There is also a standing loop with QA: when a defect reaches us from a customer, it escaped their test coverage by definition, so they review those tickets and add the gaps back to their own cases.

I have sent our leadership team a Voice-of-Customer report every month for nine months. It is not a snapshot — it tracks whether each customer-facing problem is improving month over month, which is the only version anyone argues with. When our CEO asked why a score had disappeared from one edition, I added it back the same day and made it a permanent section. Threads off those reports have turned into firmware fixes and a shared engineering project.

I have not put a number on any of this. The cross-functional benefit is real and I can describe it, but the posting asks for measurable and I would be inventing one.

5.Owning the vendor

I did the whole relationship, not just the console: selection, contract, conversation design, escalation logic, performance reviews, and eventually the decision to take the product off our site. What I got wrong is more useful than what I got right. I negotiated the price and forgot to negotiate the pace. No strict service level, no committed roadmap with any mechanism to hold them to it, and no written definition of what the product had to be able to do three and six months out. When their engineering capacity ran short and they stopped keeping up with the models available, I could be dissatisfied but I could not be specific, and a vendor conversation without specifics is just a complaint.

Day one on a new vendor relationship I would write the acceptance criteria before the signature, name the review cadence, and agree what evidence each side brings and what happens when the evidence is bad. I have since made vendor independence an architectural constraint rather than a preference: our current system selects its model through a neutral configuration layer, with a written rule that behavior must not depend on any one provider.

I have also owned a support quality-assurance platform for several years and wound its usage down as AI took over most routine auditing. Ending a vendor relationship cleanly is its own skill, and I have now done it twice.

 Where I would start

  • Read actual conversations before standing behind any inherited number, and write down what each current metric excludes.
  • Run one pilot on a high-friction journey with the success and safety criteria agreed with the partner beforehand, not after the results arrive.
  • Present an operating review that includes what did not work, because a review containing only good news trains people to stop reading it.

 Two practical notes

I have not worked on Sierra, Decagon, or Forethought. My platform partner was a small vendor and the system I built afterwards is my own, so I know the shape of the work but I would be learning a specific product.

I am not in San Francisco yet. I am willing to relocate there with my family and I am planning around the three-day in-office week rather than around an exception to it. I would need visa sponsorship.
